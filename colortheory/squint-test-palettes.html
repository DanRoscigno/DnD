Colour schemes for the painting desk

Squint Test Palettes

Seven schemes that hold together on a 32 mm model, each built as a shadow–base–highlight ramp. Drain the hue with the squint test to check the thing that actually matters.

Value beats hue

A mini is judged at arm’s length. If cloak, armour and skin all sit at the same lightness, it reads as mush no matter how nice the colours are. Split the big areas into light, mid and dark.

Two hues, plus neutrals

Roughly 60 / 30 / 10 — a dominant, a support, and one small accent that is the loudest thing on the model. Giving every pouch its own colour leaves the eye nowhere to land.

Desaturate around the accent

A slightly greyed blue cloak makes a pure gold buckle sing. Two fully saturated colours just fight. Knock the saturation out of everything you are not trying to draw the eye to.

Strips the hue from every swatch below, leaving only value. Any ramp that goes flat here will go flat on the model.

Metallics are colours

Gold is a warm yellow-orange and steel is a cool grey — count them in the scheme rather than treating them as free. A model with gold trim already has its warm accent; adding orange leather on top is one warm too many.

Tie it together

Mix a little of the accent colour into shadows elsewhere on the model, or repeat it on one small unrelated detail. Without that echo the scheme reads as separate stickers rather than one figure.

The base is part of the palette

Cool grey stone under a warm-toned model is free contrast. Green grass under a green cloak throws it away. Pick the base last, against the finished model.

Painting a party

Hold one colour constant across the whole warband — the same leather brown, the same metal — and change only the accent per model. That is what makes a group read as belonging together without looking identical.
